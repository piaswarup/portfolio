The Arctic Kinetic Sculpture

A seal, bear, and shark set in motion by chains, sprockets, and bevel gears.

Overview

The Arctic is a kinetic sculpture featuring a seal, a bear, and a shark, designed and fabricated from plywood, acrylic, PLA, and aluminum. The project explored advanced CAD techniques, a range of fabrication methods, chain and sprocket mechanics, and working as a team. I built it with a team of four in the second semester of my first year, for Mechanical Prototyping.

My role

My goals were to expand my Onshape skills, fabricate a part on a CNC and improve my CAM skills, and work with interdependent mechanisms: bevel gears and a chain drive. I designed the sprocket and chain mechanism and the overall structure, machined the bear head out of aluminum on the CNC mill, and assembled the sprocket and chain mechanism and the box structure.

Design & build

In Onshape I improved my part modeling and assembly skills and learned to use a Variable Studio and to model sheet metal.

I milled the bear head on the Tormach CNC mill, cut the L brackets on the plasma cutter and bent them on the sheet metal bender, and turned the bear arm on the wood lathe. In assembly I learned to work with chain and sprockets, including using a chain breaker and setting chain tension.

Challenges

  • Smooth chain-driven motion. We carefully calibrated chain tension and selected durable sprockets, so the motion stayed consistent without slipping or binding.
  • The shark head. Its movement was hard to simulate in CAD, which led to some interference. In assembly, we adjusted the head's angle and attachment to improve the visual effect and let the seal pass through cleanly, while keeping the mechanism working.
  • The bear arm. To make it move accurately and reliably, we iterated the arm geometry and lever length in CAD, tested prototypes, and adjusted the pivot points for smooth, controlled motion.

Results

The final sculpture assembled smoothly, with well-tensioned chains and robust sprockets. The bear arm mechanism worked as intended, and the shark's head was adjusted to enhance the visual effect.

What I learned

I learned how to turn complex CAD models into physical mechanisms and make sure moving parts like the bear arm and shark head work smoothly. Spending 45 minutes a week on CAD tutorials (assembly constraints, motion simulation, sheet metal, and Variable Studios) let me build the whole sculpture assembly and cut my CAD time significantly, and machining the bear head improved my CAM and Tormach skills a lot. Working with chains and sprockets, I learned from our spacing and sprocket misalignment issues. Timing the different mechanisms together was hard, because friction and gravity didn't behave as expected, and integrating with my teammates' mechanisms took patience and communication.

What worked well was a clear division of tasks, using CAD to plan the mechanisms before fabrication, and iterative testing. Next time, I'd prototype individual components earlier to avoid last-minute adjustments from misalignment, manage time better during assembly, and document each mechanism's motion as it develops.
