Soil Sampling Drone Attachment

A cable-suspended soil sampler that lets a fleet of drones map soil moisture without landing.

Overview

Soil moisture is one of the biggest factors in crop yield and irrigation efficiency. Variable-rate irrigation, which waters each part of a field based on measured soil moisture, can cut irrigation water use by 25–40% and raise yields by up to 15% for some crops. But it needs dense moisture maps, roughly one measurement per acre, and over 75% of farmers still rely on hand sampling and rules of thumb.

From January to May 2026, I worked at the Olin College Robotics Lab on HydroFleet, a multi-drone system for high-resolution soil moisture mapping, in collaboration with MIT. HydroFleet has four parts: a drone platform, a multi-drone path planner, a farmer dashboard, and, at its core, a cable-suspended soil sampling module that takes in-ground moisture measurements without the drone having to land. That sampling module, designed from scratch during the project, is what I worked on. We tested the system on a working 109-acre farm, Powisset Farm in Dover, MA.

My role

I was a Mechanical and Hardware Engineer on the soil sampler, as a first-year on an 11-student team. Design & build

Choosing a concept

We started from the farmers' problem: they need a reliable, affordable way to collect soil moisture data at field scale. The sampler had to probe up to 6 in deep, insert and retract its sensor without breaking it, stay within the drone's payload capacity, sample without damaging plants, and read within a 5% margin of error. We sketched three ideas: an embedded drill probe driven down a lead screw, a spring-loaded harpoon, and a sliding hammer. We chose the embedded drill probe because it was the most consistent: it wasn't clear a spring could reliably drive a harpoon 6 in into dirt, and the sliding hammer relied on force from the drone.

From mockup to Version 1

A quick physical mockup let us check the two-plate frame, the vertical supports, and how the sampler would hang before we moved to CAD. To drive the auger and sensor, we chose 3D-printed gears over chains (too heavy) and belts (they need exact ratios and tensioning), since gears are light and easy to change. We then prototyped two drive layouts: one with separate motors for the lead screw and the auger (about 610 g), and one where a single motor drives both by spinning the lead nut around a fixed screw (626 g, or 509 g without the probe, heavier mainly because it used steel rods instead of aluminum). Weight was the major constraint, so we went with the single motor, which became Version 1.

Two more problems came up in testing. At first the sensor stayed still while the auger turned around it, but the friction was too high and the sensor blocked the auger from clearing dirt. We clamped the gear, auger, and sensor together with a set screw so they spin as one, and added a slip ring so the sensor's wires don't twist. Switching to a continuous servo to save weight also lost us position feedback, so we added a distance sensor between the plates. We tried a time-of-flight sensor and an ultrasonic sensor; the time-of-flight sensor was thrown off by leaves and grass in the field, so we chose the ultrasonic.

Anchors

To drill reliably, the sampler needs a firm grip on the ground. Passive spikes stopped it twisting but not lifting, so we moved to active, servo-driven anchors. The 9 g servos were too weak to pierce the ground; switching to 8.5 kg MEUS Racing servos (about 22 g each) worked far better, but the anchors were too flimsy and broke as soon as they hit dirt. Thicker mounting screws fixed the breaking. A tapered profile, narrow at the tip and widening, made the anchor easier to dig in, but it stopped where the taper ended and could be pulled out. Making the anchor circular let the taper continue along an arc, so it digs deeper and sits perpendicular to the force from the auger, which also minimizes how much the sampler lifts.

Final design

Every change in the final design (Version 4) aimed to cut weight while keeping the sampler flat and stable on the ground. We pocketed the base to lighten it and added holes for spikes, which gave good contact with the soil before the anchors went in. We switched from a steel auger to a 3D-printed one, which dug just as well for less weight. The biggest single saving came from the sensor: we cut its long probe shorter, TIG-welded it back together, and rewired it to our own circuit, saving 47 g. Version 4 was substantially lighter than Version 3 while still reaching a 5.25 in sampling depth.

Electronics and the sampling routine

An Arduino Nano on top of the sampler runs the whole routine: extend the anchors, drive the auger and probe into the soil, take several moisture readings, retract the probe, and retract the anchors. The ultrasonic sensor checks how far the probe actually traveled; if it didn't get deep enough, because of a rock or a poor landing, the sampler aborts instead of recording junk data. Because the motors are electrically noisy and the cable is 2 m long, the drone and sampler talk over a CAN bus. The drone's 14.8 V battery powers the sampler, stepped down to 8.4 V so the servos reach full torque.

Sensor calibration

The sensor is a capacitive DSMM500 soil moisture probe; water raises the soil's dielectric constant, which raises the probe's capacitance. To log it on the drone, we dropped the original housing and read the bare probe through a custom LMC555 timer circuit. We calibrated it in soil mixed to 0%, 15%, 20%, 25%, 30%, and 50% gravimetric water content, which gave a strongly linear calibration curve (R² = 0.9969). An unmodified factory probe showed the same trend, confirming the custom circuit worked.

Results

  • Won Best Use of Drones at the Farm Robotics Challenge
  • Drone and hand-held measurements of the same soil agreed with R² = 0.947 over 10 samples
  • Sampler landing accuracy: CEP50 of 0.234 m over 11 flights (drone navigation: CEP50 of 0.43 m)
  • 5.25 in sampling depth, with the device kept below the drone's 680 g maximum payload capacity
  • A provisional patent application has been filed with our MIT partners
